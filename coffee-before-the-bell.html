Coffee Before the Bell: Why Caffeine and Boxing Have More in Common Than You Think

What research involving Olympic-level boxers tells us about caffeine, power, and the rituals of training.

Adela Tortora · Saint Michaels Coffee, LLC · 4 minute read

Coffee and boxing may not seem like an obvious combination.

One belongs at the breakfast table. The other belongs under bright lights with a bell about to ring.

But caffeine is one of the most extensively studied performance aids in sports, and researchers have even tested its effects in Olympic-level boxers.

That makes the relationship more interesting than it first appears.

Boxing Is More Than Endurance

A boxer doesn’t simply need to be “in shape.”

Boxing requires repeated bursts of explosive power, reaction time, coordination, decision-making and the ability to continue performing while fatigue builds.

A fighter can have excellent conditioning and still lose effectiveness if reaction time slows or explosive output drops.

Researchers wanted to know whether caffeine could affect some of those qualities.

Researchers Put Olympic-Level Boxers to the Test

One study examined eight male boxers from the Spanish National Olympic Team.

The experiment used a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled design.

The boxers completed performance testing after receiving caffeine and, on another occasion, a placebo.

The results were significant.

Caffeine increased peak power during a 30-second high-intensity cycling test by approximately 6 percent and average power by approximately 5 percent.

The athletes also reached peak power more quickly and demonstrated improvements in jumping performance.

That doesn’t mean caffeine suddenly turns someone into a better boxer.

But it does suggest that caffeine may influence some of the physical qualities that matter in combat sports.

What Does the Larger Research Say?

The Olympic-boxer study isn’t the only research examining caffeine and combat sports.

A systematic review and meta-analysis examining 25 studies found that caffeine supplementation could improve reaction time and some sport-specific performance measures in combat athletes.

Other reviews have found potential benefits involving strength, power and muscular endurance.

Those findings help explain why caffeine remains one of the most commonly used performance aids in sports.

But there is an important distinction.

Coffee Is Not the Same as a Measured Caffeine Supplement

Most sports-performance studies use carefully measured doses of caffeine.

A cup of coffee is different.

The caffeine content of brewed coffee can vary significantly depending on the coffee, serving size and brewing method.

Individual responses also vary.

Some people tolerate caffeine extremely well.

Others become jittery, anxious or uncomfortable.

Too much caffeine can cause nervousness, gastrointestinal discomfort and sleep disruption.

And poor sleep is certainly not a performance strategy.

So drinking more coffee does not automatically mean performing better.

Then Why Does Coffee Fit Boxing So Well?

Maybe because both have something else in common.

Routine.

Boxing is built on routines that aren’t particularly glamorous.

Roadwork.

Jump rope.

Heavy bag.

Pads.

Sparring.

Conditioning.

Then doing it again.

The same discipline that gets someone into the gym when they don’t feel like training is often built around small rituals that tell the mind it is time to work.

For many people, coffee is one of those rituals.

A cup before the day starts.

A cup before opening the gym.

A few quiet minutes before training begins.

Coffee isn’t magic.

Neither is boxing.

Both reward consistency.
