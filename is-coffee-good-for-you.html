Is Coffee Good for You? What the Science Says

A clear look at the research behind your daily cup—from liver and heart health to focus and caffeine limits.

Adela Tortora · Saint Michaels Coffee, LLC · 7 minute read

Is coffee good for you? It's one of the most-asked questions in nutrition. For a long time, the answer seemed to be no. Coffee was blamed for everything from heart trouble to stunted growth. Then researchers started following hundreds of thousands of people for years at a time. What they found surprised a lot of doctors.

For most healthy adults, moderate coffee drinking is linked with lower risk of several serious diseases. Here's what the research says, what it doesn't, and how much is too much.

Alert when it counts

This is the benefit you already feel. Caffeine blocks adenosine, a brain chemical that builds up while you're awake and makes you feel sleepy. Block it, and you stay sharper for longer.

The military has studied this closely. A committee of the National Academies reviewed the evidence for the U.S. military and concluded that 100 to 600 mg of caffeine can help maintain mental performance, "particularly in situations of sleep deprivation." In sleep-deprived people, caffeine improved:

  • Reaction speed
  • Visual and auditory vigilance (staying watchful)
  • Mental arithmetic and logical reasoning

Depending on the task and conditions, some measured effects lasted 8 to 10 hours. The committee noted these abilities can mean "a life or death situation" in the field. Its top recommendation for long operations was caffeine combined with short naps.

For anyone working nights, doubles or long calls, that's worth knowing. According to the FDA, a 12 oz cup of brewed coffee carries roughly 113 to 247 mg of caffeine. Timing matters too: in a sleep study, 400 mg of caffeine taken six hours before bed still cut total sleep by more than an hour. When you can, keep your last cup well before your planned sleep.

Your liver may be coffee's biggest fan

Of all the health links, the liver findings are among the strongest. A University of Southampton and Edinburgh study followed 494,585 people in the UK Biobank for almost 11 years. Compared with non-drinkers, coffee drinkers had:

Outcome Risk for coffee drinkers
Chronic liver disease 21% lower
Chronic or fatty liver disease 20% lower
Death from chronic liver disease 49% lower

The association was strongest at three to four cups a day. It showed up for ground, instant and decaf coffee alike, though ground coffee showed the strongest link. That raises the possibility that substances besides caffeine play a role. Researchers point to compounds such as kahweol, cafestol and chlorogenic acids.

A steadier heart and a longer life

Coffee was once treated as a heart risk. Large observational studies now report more favorable associations for many people.

A 2022 study in the European Journal of Preventive Cardiology followed 449,563 people for a median of 12.5 years. Two to three cups a day was the sweet spot:

Coffee type (2–3 cups/day) Death from any cause Cardiovascular disease
Ground 27% lower 20% lower
Decaf 14% lower 6% lower
Instant 11% lower 9% lower

It also found lower risk of irregular heartbeat (arrhythmia) with ground and instant coffee, but not decaf. The largest reported association was a 17% lower risk with four to five cups of ground coffee a day. As the researchers noted, coffee "contains more than 100 biologically active components," not just caffeine.

A larger umbrella review in The BMJ pulled together 201 observational studies and 17 clinical trials. It found three to four cups a day was most strongly linked with lower risk of death and heart disease, compared with no coffee.

Lower risk of type 2 diabetes

This is one of the most consistent findings in coffee research. A meta-analysis in Diabetes Care combined 28 studies covering more than 1.1 million people. It found:

  • About 9% lower risk of type 2 diabetes for each daily cup of caffeinated coffee
  • About 6% lower risk for each daily cup of decaf
  • 33% lower risk at six cups a day, compared with none

Because decaf showed an association too, researchers think compounds like chlorogenic acids may play a role alongside caffeine. One caution: these benefits apply to coffee, not to the sugar and syrups that often come with it.

Protecting the brain and mood

Parkinson's disease. A European study followed 184,024 people in six countries for about 13 years. The heaviest coffee drinkers had a nearly 40% lower risk of Parkinson's than non-drinkers. Blood tests taken years before diagnosis pointed to caffeine and its breakdown products as the likely reason.

Dementia. A 2026 study in JAMA tracked 131,821 people for up to 43 years. Those who drank the most caffeinated coffee had an 18% lower risk of dementia, with the best results at two to three cups a day. Decaf showed no benefit. The lead researcher called the effect "small" and said coffee is "one piece of that puzzle," not a prevention plan on its own. Earlier reviews found no clear link, so the science here is still developing.

Mood. A meta-analysis of observational studies found depression risk fell about 8% for each daily cup of coffee. That's a link, not a treatment. Anyone struggling with depression should talk to a doctor or counselor.

Stronger workouts

If you train for the job, caffeine can help. The International Society of Sports Nutrition reviewed the research and found caffeine "consistently" improves exercise performance at 3 to 6 mg per kilogram of body weight. For a 180 lb (82 kg) person, that's roughly 245 to 490 mg. Benefits showed up in:

  • Aerobic endurance (the most consistent effect)
  • Muscular endurance and strength
  • Sprinting, jumping and throwing
  • Attention and vigilance

The most common timing was about 60 minutes before exercise. The review also noted coffee works as a caffeine source. Higher doses, around 9 mg/kg, brought side effects without extra benefit.

Lower risk of two cancers

The American Institute for Cancer Research, with the World Cancer Research Fund, finds "probable evidence" that coffee lowers the risk of liver and endometrial cancers. That's their second-highest rating, a strong category of evidence in nutritional research. They found similar benefits in decaf, thanks to coffee's plant compounds.

What about acrylamide, a compound formed during roasting? AICR says "no links have been established between acrylamide in food and cancer risk for humans."

How much is too much?

More isn't always better. The FDA says most healthy adults can have up to 400 mg of caffeine a day, about two to three 12 oz cups of brewed coffee. Too much can cause a racing heart, palpitations, higher blood pressure, poor sleep, anxiety, jitters, upset stomach and headaches.

A few groups should be more careful:

  • Pregnant women: The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists advises less than 200 mg a day.
  • People on certain medications or with heart conditions, anxiety or sleep problems: Check with your doctor.
  • Women at higher risk of fracture: The BMJ review flagged this group for caution.
  • Anyone watching cholesterol: Unfiltered coffee (French press, Turkish, boiled and espresso) contains cafestol, which can raise LDL cholesterol. Paper filters remove more than 90% of it.

And remember what goes in the cup. The research is about coffee, not whipped cream, syrups and sugar. Keep the extras light.

The bottom line

For most healthy adults, moderate coffee drinking is associated with lower rates of some liver and heart outcomes, type 2 diabetes and Parkinson's; caffeine can also improve short-term alertness. None of it is a cure. Most of it comes from observational research. But the picture is more encouraging than coffee's old reputation ever suggested.
